# From Biology to Mathematics: a companion to CMU 02-680

A notebook-based self-study course that takes a **first-year biology major** from "what does
Σ mean?" to a **deep, working understanding of everything taught in CMU 02-680 *Essential
Mathematics and Statistics for Scientists*** — logic, sets and functions, sequences and graphs,
linear algebra, calculus, probability and statistics — and shows, in every module, where that
mathematics is used in biology and in the courses that build on it (02-620 *Machine Learning for
Scientists*, 02-710 *Computational Genomics*, 02-750 *Automation of Biological Research*).

The course has **29 executable Jupyter notebooks**:

- **Getting started (Modules 00–01).** Why biology needs mathematics, how to read mathematical
  notation, how to write it in LaTeX, and Python as a laboratory in which every mathematical object
  of the course has a mirror (sets, tuples, functions, vectors, matrices, random numbers).
- **The 02-680 topics (Modules 02–27).** Every topic of 02-680 taught completely inside the
  notebooks: definitions, theorems and their proofs, the classic worked examples with their numbers,
  the figures (re-drawn by code) and the common misconceptions — each summarised in 📘 *Lecture
  notes* boxes, each connected to biology in 🌉 *Bridge* boxes.
- **Capstone (Module 28).** One biological question taken all the way to a quantitative answer
  with the tools of the whole course.

Every notebook runs on a free Google Colab CPU, derives every formula step by step (each displayed
equation followed by an "*In words:*" reading), checks every hand calculation with NumPy, SymPy or
SciPy, and uses **data generated by code** — the repository stores no data sets and no pictures
(see [`DATA.md`](DATA.md)).

**Start here:** [00 · 🧬 Welcome: why biology needs mathematics](notebooks/00_welcome_math_for_biology.ipynb)

## Who it is for and what you will be able to do

**Assumed background:** high-school algebra and introductory biology. Some students will have seen
a little calculus; **no programming, linear algebra, probability or statistics is assumed.** Module 01
teaches the Python the course uses, and 🧮 *Math refresher* boxes supply every prerequisite (what Σ
means, how to expand $(a+b)^2$, what a limit is, what $e$ and $\ln$ are …) at the moment it is needed.

**After the course you will be able to** — the six objectives of 02-680, made concrete:

1. **Reason precisely with formal language.** Translate biological rules into logic (the *lac* operon,
   flow-cytometry gates), prove equivalences with truth tables, negate quantified statements, work
   with sets, functions, tuples, strings, asymptotic notation and graphs, and write short proofs
   (Modules 02–06).
2. **Perform and interpret core linear algebra on real data.** Treat an expression matrix as vectors
   and matrices; compute products, determinants, inverses and row-reduced echelon forms by hand;
   solve and characterise linear systems (null spaces of metabolic networks); decide independence,
   find bases and ranks; compute eigenvalues, eigendecompositions and the SVD and say what they mean
   for a population model or an expression data set (Modules 07–13).
3. **Use derivatives and gradients to analyse and optimise.** Differentiate and integrate the
   functions of growth, kinetics and pharmacology; find optima in one and several variables; derive
   least squares; linearise dynamical systems with the Jacobian and decide whether an equilibrium —
   of a tRNA pool or an epidemic — is stable (Modules 14–15).
4. **Apply the axioms of probability and reason about random variables, distributions and
   graphical models.** Derive the rules of probability from the axioms; apply conditioning and
   Bayes' rule to diagnostic tests and genetic risk; model counts, waiting times and measurements
   with the right distribution; work with joint, marginal and conditional distributions; read and
   compute with Bayesian networks and Markov chains (CpG islands) (Modules 16–20).
5. **Estimate parameters by maximum likelihood and Bayesian methods and quantify uncertainty.**
   Compute expectations and variances; use the law of large numbers and the central limit theorem;
   build and interpret confidence intervals; derive MLEs (allele frequencies, expression rates) and
   MAP estimates with conjugate priors and pseudocounts (Modules 21–24).
6. **Design and interpret statistical analyses, including tests and regression, and communicate
   their limitations.** Choose and run z-, t- and χ² tests; reason about Type I/II errors, power and
   sample size; control false discoveries across thousands of genes; fit and test a linear regression
   and explain what it can and cannot say about biology (Modules 25–28).

## Shared conventions

| Convention | Value |
|---|---|
| Data matrix | `X` has **N rows = samples** (cells, patients, tissue samples) and **J columns = features** (genes, SNPs, clinical variables) |
| Vectors | column vectors in formulas ($\mathbf x$, bold), 1-D NumPy arrays in code; $\mathbf x^\top\mathbf y$ is the dot product |
| Matrices | capital italic ($A$, $X$); size *rows × columns*; transpose $A^\top$ |
| Functions | $f: S \to T$; composition $(g\circ f)(x) = g(f(x))$ — $f$ is applied first |
| Graphs | path length = number of edges; a self-loop adds 2 to a degree |
| Markov chains | transition matrix rows = current state, columns = next state; every row sums to 1 |
| Logarithms | `log` is natural; log₂ for fold changes and bits, log₁₀ for Phred scores and p-value plots |
| Variance | $\sigma^2$ for a distribution; $s^2$ with $1/(n-1)$ for a sample; the MLE uses $1/n$ (the notebook always says which) |
| Normal distribution | $\mathcal N(\mu, \sigma^2)$ — the second parameter is the **variance** (SciPy's `scale` is σ) |
| Numbers | probabilities as decimals with 3–4 significant digits; exact fractions where the classic example uses fractions |
| Genotypes | 0/1/2 = number of copies of one chosen allele |
| Randomness | `SEED = 0`, `rng = np.random.default_rng(SEED)` in every notebook; simulators have their own fixed default seeds |

[`course_toolkit.py`](course_toolkit.py) (v1.0) holds the reference implementation of the shared
helpers: truth tables and logical connectives, Venn diagrams and the Jaccard index, a graph drawer
and adjacency matrices, vector plots and an exact-fraction Gauss–Jordan elimination that prints
every row operation, DNA and Markov-chain helpers, and the biology simulators. **Each notebook
contains a verbatim copy of the helpers it needs**, so every notebook runs on its own in Colab.

The course's recurring biological "characters" connect the modules: the multi-tissue infection
experiment, four immune and stromal cell types, DNA and CpG islands, an inflammation network, a
cholesterol drug trial, an insulin study, Mendel's peas and Hardy–Weinberg genotypes.

## Data: everything is generated by code

The repository stores **no data files and no pictures**. Every data set and figure is produced by
code in the notebook: the toolkit's simulators (an infection experiment in spleen, kidney and liver
with known differentially expressed genes; single-cell counts of four cell types with known rates;
Hardy–Weinberg genotypes; DNA from CpG-island and background Markov chains; a paired cholesterol
trial with a known drug effect; an insulin study with known regression coefficients), small
simulations written in the notebooks, and a few short classic tables typed into a cell and labelled
as such (Mendel's peas, Bortkiewicz's horse kicks, the genetic code, …). Every simulator returns
the hidden truth it was built from, so every method can be checked against it.
[`DATA.md`](DATA.md) documents each simulator and typed-in table, what it mimics, what truth it
returns and which modules use it.

## Running the notebooks

**Colab (recommended):** click the badge at the top of any notebook — for example
[![Open In Colab](https://colab.research.google.com/assets/colab-badge.svg)](https://colab.research.google.com/github/xulabs/edu/blob/main/math/tutorial/BiologyToMath/notebooks/00_welcome_math_for_biology.ipynb)
